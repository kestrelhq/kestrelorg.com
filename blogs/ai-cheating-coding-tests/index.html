AI Cheating on Coding Tests: What Works, What Doesn't, and How to Respond

You cannot stop candidates from using AI on an unsupervised coding test. You can decide what you allow, design tasks that reveal real skill, treat suspicious signals as reasons to talk rather than reasons to reject, and check understanding in a short live conversation. This guide shows how, and is candid about what the evidence does and does not support.

  • Decide your AI policy first: banned, allowed, or allowed with disclosure. Everything else follows from it.
  • Be sceptical of any tool that claims to detect AI-written code with high accuracy. The one independent study we found tested several detectors and found them poor.
  • Treat every integrity signal as a flag for a person to read, never a verdict.
  • Design tasks around your real work, then verify with a 10-minute walkthrough of the candidate's own code.
  • Match monitoring to the stakes, tell candidates what is recorded and why, and handle the data under DPDP.

What candidates actually do

Vendor guides describe a similar list. HackerEarth's guide to cheating in online assessments names seven patterns: an AI assistant on a second device, browser extension or overlay; remote help by screen-share or a paid helper; a proxy taking the test; leaked question banks on Telegram, Discord or GitHub; searching for canonical solutions; physical aids; and collusion. SoftwareSeni's January 2026 overview describes the mechanism behind the AI tools: capture the question by screenshot or audio, send it to a language model, and show the answer where the interviewer cannot see it.

Two points follow. Much of this happens outside the browser, where a testing platform cannot see it. And the methods change quickly, so a control that works this quarter may not work next.

How common is it? Be careful with the numbers

Headline statistics circulate widely, and most are weak:

  • A recruiting-news article, Metaintro, July 2026, reports that 38.5% of candidates were flagged for cheating across 19,368 interviews between July 2025 and January 2026. It attributes this to QBS Global, a company that sells interview-integrity services. The article does not say what "flagged" means, who was sampled or how. A flag is a suspicion, not a confirmed case.
  • SoftwareSeni cites an interviewing.io survey of 67 FAANG interviewers, of whom 81% suspected AI cheating and 33% had caught someone. That is a small sample of interviewers at large US companies, and it measures suspicion as well as detection. We have not opened the primary survey.
  • Other figures, such as "80% of candidates use AI on code tests", are quoted secondhand without a method.

What can be said is that AI-assisted answers are widespread enough that serious employers are changing their processes. How widespread is unknown, and your own pool may differ. Measure it yourself with the plan at the end.

Why "AI code detection" is a weak foundation

It is tempting to buy a detector and be done. The independent evidence is discouraging. Suh and colleagues' November 2024 paper, "An Empirical Study on Automatically Detecting AI-Generated Source Code: How Far Are We?", tested five general text detectors and one code-specific detector across several datasets and models. Mean accuracy across datasets was roughly 48% to 51% for the five text detectors and about 57% for the code-specific one. The authors concluded that the detectors "all perform poorly and lack sufficient generalizability to be practically deployed."

Two cautions apply. The study tested detectors available in 2024 on code from models of that time, and the field moves fast. And it tested those tools, not any particular product. But it is a good reason to ask any vendor, including us, for evidence of accuracy and false-positive rates before relying on an AI-detection claim. HackerEarth's guide advises treating detector output only as a trigger for a live follow-up, and says no system catches every cheater.

The cost of getting it wrong is real. A false flag can wrongly reject a good candidate, and AI-text detectors have been reported to flag non-native English writers unfairly, although HackerEarth's guide notes newer detectors have narrowed the gap.

Build in layers

No single control works. Combine several and know the limits of each.

The live walkthrough is the most reliable layer, because it tests what you care about: can this person explain and extend their own work?

Decide your AI policy first

Employers differ. News sites report that Google and Amazon tell candidates not to use AI and may disqualify those caught, that Meta has been experimenting with AI-permitted coding interviews, and that Canva requires AI use in some technical interviews. These are secondhand accounts, so check primary sources before quoting any company.

For your own team, pick one:

  • Banned. Say so plainly and decide the consequence.
  • Allowed. Test how well candidates use AI: give harder tasks, and score reasoning and review.
  • Allowed with disclosure. Ask candidates to say what they used and why.

If engineers on your team use AI every day, banning it on the test may measure something unlike the job. That is a choice for you to make, not one a tool can make for you.

Design tasks that reveal skill

  • Use realistic, context-heavy problems: debug a failing service, extend an existing function, review a change.
  • Add constraints specific to your product. Generic algorithm puzzles are the easiest for an AI to answer.
  • Ask for short written reasoning with the code: what trade-off did you make, and what would you change?
  • Refresh questions on a schedule.

SoftwareSeni reports CoderPad's CEO describing AI-assisted interviews built on codebases of 1,000 to 2,000 lines, and HackerEarth's guide argues that tasks with more context are harder to finish by copy-paste. Both are vendor views, and models are improving, so try your own tasks with an AI assistant yourself before you rely on them.

Verify with a 10-minute walkthrough

After the test, ask the candidate to explain their submission live. Five questions are enough:

  1. Walk me through your approach in your own words.
  2. Why did you choose this data structure or method?
  3. What breaks first as the input grows?
  4. Change this requirement slightly: what would you edit?
  5. Which part took you the longest, and why?

A candidate who wrote the code can usually answer these without effort, while one who copied it often cannot. Keep the tone friendly, because many honest candidates are nervous.

Handle a flag fairly

  1. Read the evidence. What exactly was flagged, and what are the harmless explanations?
  2. Look at the whole record. One paste is weak evidence. A pattern across several signals is stronger.
  3. Talk to the candidate. Use the walkthrough above.
  4. Decide with a person. A named reviewer makes the call and writes down why.
  5. Never reject on a signal alone.

Be proportionate and privacy-aware

Cameras, screen capture and browser lockdown add friction and raise privacy duties. Use them where the stakes justify it, such as late rounds or senior roles. Tell candidates what is recorded, why, how long it is kept and who sees it. India's DPDP Act, with main duties expected around May 2027, applies to this personal data. Whether and how it covers job applicants is debated, so take legal advice on notices, retention and consent.

A 30-day plan to measure your own problem

Week 1. Write your AI policy and publish it to candidates.

Weeks 2 to 3. Add the walkthrough for every candidate who reaches the next round. Log the outcome and any integrity signals.

Week 4. Count how many flags there were, how many were confirmed in conversation and how many were harmless. Compare test scores with walkthrough performance.

If scores and walkthroughs rarely agree, the test is measuring something else. Change the tasks, not just the monitoring.

Frequently asked questions

Can AI-generated code be reliably detected?

Not at present, on the independent evidence we found. A 2024 study found several detectors performed poorly and generalised badly. Treat detection output as a prompt for a conversation.

Should I ban AI on coding tests?

It is your call. Banning is hard to enforce on an unsupervised test, and allowing it changes what you test. Whichever you choose, say it clearly to candidates.

What is the best way to catch AI-assisted answers?

A short live walkthrough of the candidate's own code. It tests understanding, which a copied answer rarely has.

Is proctoring worth it?

For high-stakes rounds it can raise the effort of cheating, but it adds friction and privacy duties and will not catch a second device. Use it in proportion.

Can I reject a candidate based on a cheating flag?

We would not. A flag is a suspicion. Review the evidence, talk to the candidate and let a named person decide.
