Most lists of HackerRank alternatives give you ten to twenty names and a feature grid which helps if you already know what you need, but most startups do not. The choice usually comes down to questions the lists skip: how many candidates you actually test, whether your test still tells you anything when candidates use AI, and what happens to your budget when the invoice is in US dollars.

This guide starts with the job you need the tool to do, compares five types of alternatives, then gives you a weighted scorecard and a one role bake off to settle the decision with your own data. It is written for engineering-led teams in India, so it covers GST, DPDP and candidate conditions where they matter. It is not a case against HackerRank. For some teams staying is the right call, and a section below says when. Sources were checked in October 2026.

Start with the job, not the tool

Technical hiring teams usually ask a coding assessment tool to do four separate jobs:

  1. Screen volume. Cut a few hundred applicants down to a short list with an automated test.
  2. Run live interviews. Give an interviewer and a candidate a shared editor.
  3. Protect the result. Make sure the person who took the test did the work, whether the threat is copied code, AI help or impersonation.
  4. Feed the decision. Put the result next to the resume, the interviews and the offer so a hiring manager can act on it.

No tool is equally good at all four. If a list says one is, ask which job it was tested on. Write down which of the four you use today and which you would miss. That list is your real requirement.

Should you leave HackerRank at all?

Check before you shortlist anything, because the product has changed. According to HackerRank’s own help centre, it now offers an AI assistant inside the candidate editor for screening and interviews, AI plagiarism detection, webcam and screenshot proctoring flags, and a voice based AI interviewer. Some of these are labelled as AI add ons, so check what your own plan includes. If you last compared tools a year ago, an “alternative” that matches your old HackerRank setup may now be behind the current product.

How do customers rate it? On Capterra India, HackerRank scores 4.6 out of 5 from 85 reviews at the time of writing. In the reviews visible on that page, users praise realistic problems, monitoring and plagiarism flags, live interviews and ATS integrations. The recurring complaints are cost for small teams, an in browser editor that feels limiting, slow performance and some strong candidates declining the test.

Cost is the complaint most worth checking against your own numbers. A March 2026 third party pricing review described plans billed annually and priced in dollars, with a single user licence on the entry plan and a yearly cap on candidate attempts. Pricing changes often, so confirm current terms with the vendor. What you need to settle is whether the cap and the currency suit a team that hires in bursts.

Pricing models differ more than prices do. The same Rework comparison shows pay per invite, per candidate, per seat and per hire models. A tool that looks cheaper per seat can cost more at your volume, so compare total annual cost on the same number of candidates tested.

Seven questions to decide

1. How many candidates do you actually test?

Count the candidates you tested in your last two hiring rounds and the hires they produced. Then calculate:

Cost per hire from assessments = (annual tool cost + engineer review hours × hourly cost) ÷ hires made

Use the vendor’s real invoice, in rupees, including GST. A seat based plan suits steady hiring, and a per candidate or per invite plan suits bursts. If the plan is capped annually, include the cost of unused attempts.

2. Does the test measure something like the job?

The research on selection methods is more nuanced than “coding tests predict performance.” In a 2022 meta analysis in the Journal of Applied Psychology, Sackett and colleagues revised earlier estimates. A SIOP summary of that paper reports operational validities of .42 for structured interviews, .40 for job knowledge tests, .33 for work sample tests and .31 for cognitive ability. These are averages across many jobs, not claims about any product. They do suggest that no single format wins, and that pairing a job relevant task with a structured conversation is sensible.

A timed algorithm puzzle measures one slice of engineering skill, while a task drawn from your own codebase is closer to a work sample. Decide which you need, and ask each vendor how you would build it.

3. Does it still work when candidates use AI?

Two strategies exist: prohibit and detect, or allow and observe. HackerRank itself offers both, with an AI assistant in its editor and plagiarism detection. In an article on AI in interviews, it proposes four policy levels (allowed, limited, flagged, banned) and describes them as industry best practice, not official policy. Whichever you choose, tell candidates up front and test the tool under that policy.

Here is a practical check. Have one of your engineers take the test with an AI assistant open. If the result looks the same as an unaided run, the test is not separating anyone. Whichever vendor you pick, add a short live discussion of the submission in which the candidate explains their choices.

4. What does it record, and how is it handled under DPDP?

Proctoring often means webcam, screen and behavioural data. Ask what is recorded, whether software or a person judges it, how long it is kept and how candidates are told. The DPDP Rules were notified in November 2025, and under the phased schedule the main duties on notice, consent and security start around 13 May 2027. Check the current position before relying on that date, and take legal advice on your use case.

5. What is it like for candidates?

Take the test yourself on a laptop and a home connection like your candidates’, and time it. The Capterra reviews mention strong candidates declining the test, a reminder that a long or clumsy assessment costs you applicants before it screens anyone. Ask each vendor for completion rates on tests of similar length.

6. Where do results land?

A score that lives in a separate dashboard gets forgotten. Check that the tool integrates with the applicant tracking system you actually use, not just the global ones on its integration page, and that you can export results if you leave.

7. How are you billed?

Ask whether you can pay in rupees against a GST invoice, whether an annual prepayment is required, and what happens to unused candidate attempts. These questions rarely appear in comparison lists and often decide the real cost for an Indian startup.

Compare shortlisted tools with a scorecard

Score each tool from 0 to 5 on each criterion, multiply by its weight and divide by 5, then add the results. The maximum is 100.

Run a one role bake off

Before you sign, spend a week on one open role:

  1. Have two of your own engineers take each shortlisted test, once without AI and once with it.
  2. Review ten real submissions by hand and compare your ranking with the tool’s automatic score.
  3. Search for the question text online to see whether it has leaked.
  4. Time the candidate flows end to end, and note every point where a candidate could get stuck.
  5. Ask for a quote in rupees, with GST, at your expected volume.

Then score the tools on the table above using what you saw.

When staying with HackerRank makes sense

Stay if you test large numbers of candidates and its per attempt or per seat structure fits that volume, if you rely on its question library, proctoring and ATS integrations, and if your engineers already trust its scores. In that case the better move is often to fix your process (a live follow up, a clear AI policy, a shorter test) rather than the tool.

Frequently asked questions

Should candidates be allowed to use AI in coding tests?

It is a policy choice, not a technical one. Decide whether AI use is allowed, limited, flagged or banned for each stage, tell candidates before they start, and make sure the test and any monitoring match the policy. Whatever you choose, a short live discussion of the submission is the strongest check.

Are take home projects better than timed tests?

Real repository tasks are closer to day to day work, which makes them a better work sample. They also take more candidate time and are the easiest format to hand to an AI, so pair them with a conversation about the submission.

Do I need proctoring?

Only if the risk justifies the cost in candidate trust and data handling. If you record a webcam or screen, decide who reviews it and how long you keep it, tell candidates, and consider whether a live follow up would give you the same assurance with less data.

Is HackerEarth or Codility a better choice than HackerRank?

Neither is better in general. Rework’s comparison describes HackerEarth as strongest for hackathons and developer sourcing, and Codility for protecting tasks from leaking and building tasks from your own codebase. Use the scorecard above to test them against your own needs.
