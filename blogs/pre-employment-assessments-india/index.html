Pre-Employment Assessments in India: Which Types to Use, and How to Keep Them Fair

The test catalogue is the wrong place to start. Start with the job: what the person will actually do in their first six months, and which of those tasks a test could sample. Then choose the fewest tests that add real information, set cut-offs carefully, check results by group, and tell candidates what to expect. This guide covers the main test types, what the evidence says about them, and how to keep a process defensible in India.

  • Pick tests that sample the work or the knowledge the job needs. Job-related tests tend to be stronger predictors than general ones, but none replaces a structured interview.
  • Use as few tests as you can. Each should answer a question the others do not.
  • Treat scores as one input to a human decision, not an automatic filter.
  • Check pass rates by group, offer accessible alternatives, and keep a record of why each test is in the process.
  • Ask vendors for validation evidence. If they have none, plan a small local check.

Start from the job

List the five to eight tasks that matter most in the role, then ask of each whether a test could sample it. A support agent writes replies and handles upset customers. An analyst cleans data and explains findings. A backend engineer designs and debugs services. If you cannot name the tasks, you cannot choose a valid test.

The main test types

Testlify's catalogue article, a vendor piece, lists ten types, from role-specific and programming tests to psychometric, situational judgment, cognitive, emotional intelligence, language, typing and blue-collar tests. They group into a handful of families:

PersonalityBehavioural tendencies and work styleUseful as a supplementHigher evidence bar; easy to over-read; do not use as sole filter

On the evidence: in the 2022 re-analysis by Sackett and colleagues, summarised in SIOP's TIP and repeated in Rework's 2026 guide to AI pre-employment assessments, operational validity was about .42 for structured interviews, .40 for job-knowledge tests, .38 for biodata, .33 for work samples and .31 for cognitive ability. These are averages across studies of selection methods in general, not guarantees for any one test. They suggest that job-related knowledge and work samples are at least as informative as general cognitive tests, and that a well-run structured interview adds more than any single test. Rework also notes that a label such as “AI” or “game-based” says how a score is produced, not whether it predicts anything.

How many tests?

More tests do not mean better decisions. Each extra test costs candidate time, increases drop-off and adds another score to interpret. A useful rule: if two tests would rank people the same way, keep the cheaper one.

Sample structures to adapt rather than copy:

  • Engineering role. Resume screen, a short job-knowledge test, a coding task, then a structured interview.
  • Non-engineering role (analyst, support, success). Resume screen, a role-competency test, a work sample or scenario, then a structured interview.
  • High-volume hiring. A short, accessible skills or situational test early, then human review of those near the cut-off.

Be careful with cut-offs

A pass mark is a hiring decision. Avoid a single hard line where you can. Use bands of clear pass, review and clear fail, and have a person read the review band. The same logic applies to resume screening.

Fairness: what to check

  • Link tests to the job. Write down why each test exists and what task it samples. Under the US Uniform Guidelines, which many practitioners use as a reference even outside the US, job-relatedness is the core question.
  • Check results by group. The four-fifths rule compares each group's pass rate with the highest group's rate and flags ratios below 0.80. It is a screening heuristic from US practice, not a safe harbour and not Indian law, and it ignores sample size (Prevue HR). Use it as an internal alarm, and only where you can lawfully and reliably measure groups.
  • Check language and format. A test written in idiomatic English can measure reading speed or exposure rather than the skill you want. Use plain language, and consider whether the job really needs high-level English.
  • Offer accessible alternatives. Extra time, keyboard-only use and screen-reader support. Ask the vendor for these in writing.
  • Do not rely on personality results alone. If you use them, validate against your own roles and keep them out of automatic rejections.
  • Keep humans in the decision. Rework's guidance, which we share, is to use scores as one input to structured, human-reviewed decisions and to monitor pass rates by group regularly.
  • If you use a vendor, you are usually still answerable for the process. Ask for validation studies and group-level results, not just feature lists.

The India angle

  • Volume. Campus drives and mass hiring can put thousands of candidates through a test in days. Plan capacity, support and a fair retake policy before launch.
  • Devices and connectivity. Many candidates test on shared devices or variable connections. Ask whether work autosaves and what happens if a session drops.
  • Language. Candidates come from varied educational backgrounds and languages. Do not assume the test language is neutral.
  • Data. Scores, recordings and answers are personal data. India's DPDP Act, with main duties expected around May 2027, applies. Tell candidates what you collect and why, set retention periods, and take legal advice on notices and consent, since how the Act applies to applicants is still debated.

These points are reasoning from how Indian hiring works, not findings from India-specific assessment research, which we did not find.

Ask vendors these eight questions

  1. What job tasks or knowledge does each test sample?
  2. What validation evidence exists, for which jobs and populations?
  3. Can you show pass rates by group for similar customers?
  4. How are cut-offs set, and can we use bands?
  5. How are questions created, reviewed and refreshed?
  6. What accessibility options exist?
  7. Where is candidate data stored, for how long, and how is it deleted?
  8. How do you detect cheating, and what happens to a flag?

Write down the answers. A vague answer is an answer.

A small local check

If the vendor has no validation for roles like yours, run a simple check. Ask current employees, a mix of strong and average performers, to take the test and see whether scores line up with the performance picture you already have. Samples of 15 to 30 people are too small to prove anything, but they can reveal a test that is badly off for your roles. Repeat after a few hires.

Frequently asked questions

What are pre-employment assessments?

Tests used before a hiring decision to measure skills, knowledge, reasoning or work style. Common types are job-knowledge tests, work samples, cognitive tests and situational judgment tests.

Which type predicts job performance best?

In the 2022 Sackett re-analysis, structured interviews (.42) and job-knowledge tests (.40) led, ahead of work samples (.33) and cognitive ability (.31). These are averages, not guarantees.

Are pre-employment tests legal in India?

We found no India-specific ban. The DPDP Act affects how you handle the data. Take legal advice for your situation.

Should I use personality tests?

Use with care. They face a higher evidence bar, raise privacy questions and are easy to over-read. Do not use them as a sole basis for rejection.

How long should an assessment be?

As short as it can be while still sampling the job. Measure completion and drop-off in your pilot.

Do I need to check for bias?

Yes. Compare pass rates across groups you can lawfully and reliably measure, and review them after any change to tests or job.
